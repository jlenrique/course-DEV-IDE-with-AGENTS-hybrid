# PHS 620 Week 4 — 15-minute presentation narration script (v01)

## Standing on this script

This is **source-side narration** — the draft that stands in place of the
Notion-style source material the pipeline ingests for other courses. It is not
the final voice-over. The final narration is authored against approved stills,
so that on-screen references can be verified rather than assumed. Treat the
words here as the intended content and register, not as locked delivery copy.

## Audience

Accomplished adult professionals with advanced degrees and significant
workplace roles in healthcare. Most have real teaching and training experience
inside their organizations. Nearly all are **novices in formal instructional
design** and in software or educational-software development. They are not
students in the ordinary sense; they are colleagues learning an unfamiliar
craft.

## Voice constraints (operator, 2026-09-13)

1. **No jargon.** Where a technical term is unavoidable, say the plain meaning
   instead. Do not use "scaffold," "formative," "modality," or "alignment
   chain" as though they were shared vocabulary.
2. **Appreciative inquiry in spirit.** Begin from what the audience already does
   well and connect the new work to it. Do not frame the week as correcting a
   deficit.
3. **Respect intelligence, experience, and professional motivation.** Assume
   competence. Explain the unfamiliar without explaining the obvious.
4. **Complete sentences throughout.** No fragments.
5. **No marketing language, no hype, no catchy one-liners.** Nothing that would
   read as a slogan if lifted out of context.
6. **Business casual, academic setting, serious subject.** The subject is
   reliably educating other people. The stance is colleagues working on a shared
   problem, not an expert instructing novices.

## Card 1 — Orientation · 50s

Welcome to Week 4. Before we look at what this week asks of you, it is worth
naming where you already are. Over the past two weeks you built a defensible
account of your learners, your setting, and one focal lesson, and then you
turned that account into a plan, with objectives, evidence, sequence, and access
all lined up. That work stands. Nothing this week starts over. What you have is
a cumulative Lesson Blueprint, and this week opens Product 3 by touching only
two of its six panels. On screen you can see the whole Blueprint, with Panels 5
and 6 highlighted. Those are the two you will revise.

## Card 2 — The three-week arc · 35s

It helps to see three weeks at once. In Week 3 you planned the lesson. This week
you rehearse one small part of it, live, with your colleagues in the room. Next
week you test that same part again, and you build the one interactive piece your
lesson needs. Each week does a single job. This week's job is the first occasion
on which your design meets actual people.

## Card 3 — Only one component becomes real · 40s

Your Blueprint registers four components. This week only one of them becomes
real, and that is the live instructional segment. The other three — the
interactive element, the assessment, and the independent presentation — stay
exactly as they are, marked as plans. I want to be direct about why that
matters. It is not a limitation, and it is not the assignment being lenient. It
is a deliberate boundary, and its purpose is to keep this week small enough that
you can actually learn something from it.

## Card 4 — The five moves · 55s

So what are you rehearsing? Five minutes of teaching that completes one small
cycle. You can see the cycle on screen. You present something, which might be
an instruction, a question, a case, or a situation. You offer some support for
it, such as a model, an example, or a partial answer — whatever makes the task
possible. A learner makes a brief attempt. You respond to that particular
attempt with one useful observation. And then the same learner tries again,
using what you just said. That last move is the one most often left out. If it
is missing, you have given a short talk rather than run a teaching cycle.

## Card 5 — An interactive lecture is acceptable · 45s

You may be wondering what form this has to take. An interactive lecture is
entirely acceptable. Most of you have run something close to it at work: a
briefing, an in-service, a short training block in which you asked people to try
something. That experience transfers directly to this assignment. What you are
doing is managing a room through one cycle. The form is not what is being
examined. Whether the cycle finishes is.

## Card 6 — You are the mechanism this week · 55s

There is something worth knowing before you begin preparing. You do not need to
build anything. Whatever a finished version of your lesson might eventually do
automatically — presenting the case, judging the response, deciding what comes
next — you do by hand this week, yourself. In design work this is sometimes
called working from behind the curtain, where a person supplies what a system
will later supply. On screen you can see both sides of that curtain. For your
rehearsal, you are the mechanism. That is not a shortcut and it is not a lesser
version of the work. It is how you find out whether the design is sound before
you spend anything building it.

## Card 7 — Choosing your five minutes · 45s

Which five minutes should you choose? Look at your own Panels 3 and 4, at the
sequence you have already written. Find the phase in which you planned to
explain or demonstrate something before learners practice on their own. On
screen, that phase is circled. In most designs it is where five minutes fits
without borrowing time from the practice that comes afterward. You are not
compressing the whole lesson into five minutes. You are lifting one part out of
it.

## Card 8 — Prepare only what the five minutes uses · 50s

Prepare only the materials those five minutes will actually consume. On screen,
the left column shows what you are likely to need: the text or case your
learners will look at, whatever criteria you are holding them to, something to
keep time, and a clear way for people to respond. The right column shows what
you do not need this week. One item on the left deserves emphasis. A person who
cannot take part the usual way needs a different route that asks the same thing
of them. That route belongs to your design; it is not an accommodation added
afterward.

## Card 9 — Decide what you want to learn · 35s

Before you teach, decide what you want the rehearsal to tell you. Name your
lesson, the objective it serves, the version you are showing, and the single
question you want your colleagues to help you answer. Thirty seconds is enough
for all of that. Say it before the clock starts, because those thirty seconds
are not part of your five minutes.

## Card 10 — Maya's first ninety seconds · 55s

Let us walk through an example, since the week provides one. Maya Rivera is a
fictional colleague who trains community health workers, and her materials lay
out five minutes against a clock. In her first thirty seconds she names what the
segment is for and states the limit of the role, which is that her learners do
not diagnose and do not advise on medication. Then she reads one line from a
case — "I do not want anyone at work to know" — and asks her learners to choose
among three possible responses. And then she stops talking for twenty seconds.
That pause is deliberate, and we will return to why.

## Card 11 — Explaining, modeling, and staying in bounds · 55s

Over the next minute she explains why asking a question reveals something that
restating the facts would not, and she models a single sentence: "What concerns
you most about the next step?" She reads the reply, which turns out to be about
privacy and about missing a shift, and she puts that concern back into plain
words. Then she connects it to the safe version of the response. She declines to
give medical advice, arranges the nurse contact her protocol requires, offers
two private ways to follow up, and confirms which one the person prefers.
Notice that she is modeling the behavior rather than talking about it.

## Card 12 — The cycle completing, and one honest word · 60s

The final stretch is the cycle closing. She invites everyone to compose an
opening sentence, then asks one colleague to say theirs aloud. She names what
worked in it and offers one specific next move. Then she asks that same person
to try the sentence again, using what she just said. Roughly twenty seconds for
each step. She closes by connecting what happened to the practice that comes
later in her lesson. One more detail deserves your attention. In her first
rehearsal she gave the answer away before anyone could choose, so she added the
pause. Her Blueprint now records that component as revised rather than tested,
because the change itself has not yet been observed. That distinction will
matter for your own entry.

## Card 13 — The rhythm of the session · 50s

The rehearsal follows a simple rhythm, which you can see on screen. Five minutes
of teaching, two minutes of feedback, and one minute to change over. Your
colleagues will hold you to the five minutes, and that is a genuine courtesy,
because the limit is what keeps the exercise honest. Everything else happens
outside the teaching window. Your framing beforehand, the discussion afterward,
and any revision you make to your Blueprint all sit outside those five minutes.

## Card 14 — How you run it is your decision · 55s

How you run those five minutes is your decision. On screen you can see several
options side by side, and none of them is preferred. Slides are fine. A shared
document is fine. A website, a chat window, a quick poll, a shared screen — all
of them are fine, on their own or in combination. What matters is only this. You
need a way to get a response back from a person, a way to say something specific
about that particular response, and somewhere for them to try again. If your
arrangement does those three things, it is the right arrangement.

## Card 15 — Your colleagues do two things, in order · 55s

After you teach, your colleagues do two different things, and the order matters.
First they were your learners, so they can tell you what it was like to be on
the receiving end of your teaching. Then they become designers again and discuss
the design itself. Both perspectives are useful, and taking them in that order
protects the first one. When they give you feedback, the shared protocol asks
for a particular shape, which you can see on screen: I observed something
specific, it matters for this reason, and here is one proportionate thing to
change or test next. A single concrete observation is enough. It does not need
to be a study.

## Card 16 — Recording one decision accurately · 55s

Then you record one decision. Panel 5 carries the current state of the segment
you just taught, and Panel 6 carries one entry in three parts: what you
observed, what you decided, and what that means for the lesson. Deciding to keep
your design as it is counts as a decision, provided you say why. On screen you
can see the words available to you for describing a component's state. Use
tested only when something was genuinely tried and watched. If you changed
something and have not yet seen the change in use, it is revised. Being accurate
here is not paperwork. It is the difference between evidence and assertion.

## Card 17 — The same cycle, running at scale · 60s

I want to show you this cycle running somewhere many of you have already seen
it. On the left is an exercise from a language-learning application. It presents
a task, and it offers support, because the words you might need are supplied and
you can hover over any of them to check the meaning. You make an attempt. On the
right is what came back after a wrong answer. Your attempt is still visible, and
the correct version sits underneath it. Those are the same five moves we have
been discussing, running many thousands of times a day. The only difference is
that a system is performing them. This week you perform them yourself, by hand,
which is precisely why five minutes is a reasonable amount to take on. Later in
the course we look at what building that machinery involves.

## Card 18 — Other shapes, and what comes next · 45s

Three other shapes fit five manual minutes just as well. You can ask one
well-placed question inside an otherwise ordinary explanation. You can
demonstrate something and have a colleague attempt it immediately afterward. Or
you can show a completed example and have someone draft one line of their own.
To close: there is nothing to upload this week. Your evidence carries forward
into Week 6, and next week you test this same cycle again. If you find yourself
curious afterward, spend ten minutes in an application like the one we just
looked at, from the learner's side. We return to that idea in the second half of
the course.

## Deliberate omissions

- The four module learning objectives are never read aloud or shown. They shape
  the week's job; printing them would violate the pairing lock.
- The Sunday deadline is never spoken. It is a Canvas fact, not a recording fact.
- No claim is made that the language application explains its reasoning. The
  captured frames show a correction, not a generated explanation, so the
  narration claims only that the system answers the specific attempt.
- No product name, pricing tier, or interface feature is named in card 17, so
  the card does not age with the application.
